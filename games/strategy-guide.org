agents across two tournament rounds. Round 1 (100 games) established foundational
strategy. Round 2 (100 games) pressure-tested, refined, and overturned many
Round 1 conclusions through deeper theory-informed play.

* The Fundamentals

** Win Condition

Place all 5 forts on the board. This is a race -- there is no territorial
victory condition. Territory only matters because you need 5+ squares to
fortify, and you need fort supply (5 minus forts on board minus banked moves).

** Turn Structure

Each turn gives you 2 base actions. Options for each action:
- Move in a cardinal direction (n/s/e/w)
- Fortify your current square (f) -- requires 5+ territory and fort supply > 0
- Pass/end turn (x) -- available as 2nd action only
- Bank a move (k) -- available as 2nd action only, stores a move for later

Important rule: pass (x) and bank (k) *cannot be the first action* of a turn.
The first action must be a directional move or fortify.

Banked moves let you take extra actions on future turns (3, 4, 5+ action turns).

** The Territory Threshold

The single most important number in Terratri is *5*. You cannot place forts
until you control 5 territory squares (the =.= and =_= markers on the board --
your pawn position does not count toward this total). Every strategic decision
should be evaluated against this threshold:
- Am I moving toward 5 territory?
- Will this move cost me territory (dropping below 5)?
- Can I deny my opponent 5 territory?

* Opening Theory

** Red Openings (Ranked)

Red moves first from c1 (center bottom). 200 games have produced a clear
hierarchy:

*** S+ Tier: =wn= (the optimal Red opening)

=wn= (west-north): c1->b1->b2. This is the *universal optimal Red opening*,
discovered in Batch 10 championship games. It works because:

1. Creates territory on the b-column (b1, b2) away from the c-column
2. Followed by =en= (b2->c2->c3), gives Red 5 territory (c1, b1, b2, c2, c3)
3. Red's fort chain then runs through d3->d4->d5->c5->c4 -- entirely through
   Blue's standard expansion path
4. No overlap between Red's territory base and fort chain (self-fueling)
5. Beats both Blue SES and Blue SWS responses

The complete optimal Red line: =wn|...|en|...|ef|...|nf|...|nf|...|wf|...|sf|=

Game 200 verified: Red wins Turn 7 with 5 forts to Blue's 1.

*** A Tier: North-first openings (7-8 turn wins)
- =ne= (north-east): c1->c2->d2. Clean center-east development.
- =en= (east-north): c1->d1->d2. Develops the east flank smoothly.

These reach 5 territory by Turn 3 but are slightly inferior to =wn= because
the c-column territory overlaps with optimal fort placement paths.

*** B Tier: Viable but suboptimal
- =nn= (north-north): c1->c2->c3. Fast center control but *fatally vulnerable*
  to Blue SWS. Games 184, 187, 192 all show Blue winning against =nn=.
- =ee= (east-east): c1->d1->e1. Edge play, efficient but narrow.
- =nw= (north-west): c1->c2->b2. Similar to =wn= but c2 territory overlaps fort path.

*** F Tier: Avoid
- =ew= / =we= / any backtracking: Returns to a visited square. Pure tempo waste.
- Any banking opening (=nk=, =ek=): Round 2 proved banking as Red always loses tempo.

** Blue Openings

Blue starts at c5 (center top) with 1 banked move, giving 3 actions on Turn 1.

*** S Tier: Standard SES (best overall Blue response)

=SES= (south-east-south): c5->c4->d4->d3. Against the optimal Red =wn=, this
is Blue's best response. It loses in 7 turns but puts up the most competitive
fight, creating territory on the d-column that Red must march through.

*** A Tier: Situational counters

=SWS= (south-west-south): c5->c4->b4->b3. *Beats Red =nn=* by blocking Red's
optimal =wn= path. Blue's best choice when Red opens =nn= (Games 184, 187, 192).
Loses to Red =wn= because Red already occupies the b-column.

*** B Tier: Adaptive BlueOpt

=SK= (south-bank) Turn 1, then burst Turn 2. Round 2 refined this into
*Adaptive BlueOpt*: choose burst direction based on Red's opening trail.

Best burst per Red opening:
- vs =nn= openings: =SSWS= (7-turn wins -- fastest Blue strategy found)
- vs =en= openings: =SSES= (8 turns, overwrites d-column)
- vs =wn= openings: =WSSW= (8 turns, overwrites b/a-columns)

The burst must target Red's specific trail to overwrite territory. Non-adaptive
bursts fail against c-column-avoiding Red.

However, Batch 10 showed that *BlueOpt is slightly slower than standard SES*
against optimal Red play. The banking cost (1 fort supply) and delayed expansion
negate the burst advantage. BlueOpt remains strong against suboptimal Red.

*** C Tier: Double-Fort (FEF/FWF)

Places 2 forts in one turn using the banked move. Viable against unprepared Red
but countered by blocking forts at c4 (Game 75, 80). Banking cost negates the
fort advantage at championship level (Batch 10).

** Opening Principles (Revised)

1. *Red should play =wn=.* The universal optimal opening. Avoids c-column overlap.
2. *Blue should play SES against =wn=.* The most competitive losing response.
3. *Blue should play SWS against =nn=.* Exploits Red's c-column vulnerability.
4. *Blue should bank (SK) only against suboptimal Red.* Standard expansion beats
   BlueOpt when Red plays optimally.
5. *Never bank as Red.* Every game where Red banked in Round 2 lost tempo.

* Fortification Strategy

** When to Fortify

Fortify as soon as you have 5+ territory and fort supply > 0. Every turn you
delay fortification, your opponent can place a fort and pull ahead in the race.

#+begin_quote
"Territory without forts is ephemeral." -- Game 18 (Batch 1)
#+end_quote

** The Fort-Move Pattern

The optimal endgame technique is alternating move-fortify:
- =ef= (east, fortify), =nf= (north, fortify), =sf= (south, fortify)
- Or =fe= (fortify, east), =fn= (fortify, north), etc.

This places one fort per turn while positioning for the next.

** The Key Discovery: Through-Territory Forting

The single most important strategic discovery across 200 games:

#+begin_quote
*Build forts on your opponent's territory, not your own.*
#+end_quote

This is the principle of *self-fueling vs self-consuming* forts (Batch 8):

*** Self-fueling forts (S Tier)
Fort on enemy or unclaimed territory. Each fort placed:
- Scores toward your 5-fort win condition
- Steals an opponent territory square (denial as free side effect)
- Preserves your own territory count above the fortification threshold

Red's optimal fort chain (d3->d4->d5->c5->c4) runs *entirely through Blue's
standard territory*, denying Blue 1 square with every fort placed. By Turn 5,
Blue has been pushed below 5 territory and cannot fort at all.

*** Self-consuming forts (C Tier)
Fort on your own territory. Each fort placed:
- Scores toward your 5-fort win condition
- BUT consumes one of your own territory markers
- Risks dropping yourself below the 5-territory threshold

Column sweeps along your own expansion axis are the classic self-consuming
mistake. Red forting along c1->c2->c3 eats into Red's own dot trail.

*** Fort Placement Hierarchy

1. *Self-fueling > self-consuming*: Always prefer enemy/unclaimed squares.
2. *Straight-line > diagonal*: Fort-move-fort in one direction = 2 actions/fort.
   Diagonal requires alternating, costing 3+ actions/fort.
3. *Row sweep > column sweep*: Rows cross both players' territory; columns run
   parallel to expansion axes and tend to self-consume.
4. *Connected forts > scattered*: Fort walls block opponent movement AND score.
5. *Adaptive > forced geometry*: Evaluate which squares give the best denial
   bonus rather than committing to a fixed geometric pattern.

** Fort Blocking

Blocking forts disrupt opponent plans at the cost of 1 tempo. The main
applications:

- *c4 block*: Counters Blue's FEF double-fort by cutting the northward column.
- *Row wall*: A connected row of 3+ forts bisects the board and blocks movement.
  Blue's c-column fort wall (c2-c3-c4-c5) is the strongest structural defense.
- *Fort-denial*: Placing a fort on the opponent's planned fort path forces them
  to reroute, costing 2+ opponent turns for your 1 tempo investment.

** Comeback Potential

Round 2 conclusively proved: *a player 2+ forts behind cannot recover* against
competent play (Batch 8). Two forts = 3-4 tempi deficit, which is insurmountable.
The maximum recoverable deficit is 1 fort, achievable only through aggressive
territory invasion that drops the opponent below the 5-territory threshold.

* Banking Strategy

** Banking Overview

Banking (k/K) stores an action for later use. Each banked move costs 1 fort
supply. Blue starts with 1 free banked move.

| Banked | Turn Actions | Fort Supply Cost |
|--------+--------------+------------------|
|      0 |            2 |                0 |
|      1 |            3 |                1 |
|      2 |            4 |                2 |
|      3 |            5 |                3 |
|      4 |            6 |                4 |

** Round 2 Verdict: Banking is Mostly Suboptimal

Round 1 treated banking as a tactical option. Round 2 proved it is *almost
always suboptimal*:

- *Red should never bank.* Both actions should be used for expansion/fortification.
  Every banking game for Red in Batches 8 and 10 lost tempo. The =nk= counter to
  BlueOpt (Batch 6) showed marginal success (~55%) but is strictly worse than the
  =wn= opening which beats BlueOpt without banking.

- *Blue's free bank is key.* Blue starts with 1 banked move (3 actions Turn 1).
  This is Blue's structural compensation for going second.

- *Additional Blue banking (SK) is situational.* Against suboptimal Red (=nn=
  openings), the BlueOpt SK->burst is the fastest Blue strategy (7-turn SSWS).
  Against optimal Red (=wn=), standard SES without additional banking is stronger.

** When Banking Still Works

1. *Blue SK against Red =nn=*: The adaptive BlueOpt burst overwrites Red's
   c-column trail. This is the one remaining high-value banking play.
2. *Desperation*: When already losing, banking for a multi-fort burst turn is
   the only comeback mechanism. It rarely works but is the best bad option.

** Banking Traps (Confirmed by Round 2)

1. *Banking too long*: Banking 4+ turns gives the opponent free forts.
2. *Fort supply exhaustion*: 5 banked = 0 supply = no forts possible.
3. *Delayed burst is strictly inferior*: Batch 7 proved that banking beyond 1
   extra move (Blue SK) never recovers the lost tempo.
4. *Triple-fort is impossible*: Requires 5+ banked moves, by which time the
   opponent has 3-4 forts already (Batch 7).

* Territory Control

** Spatial Patterns

*** Edge/Column play
Moving along an edge creates linear territory easy to fortify. Efficient but
cedes the center.

*** Center play
Center control (c3) is positionally strong but has the oscillation trap: the
center has only 4-5 reachable squares without backtracking. Game 90 showed the
"Center" strategy stuck in infinite oscillation.

*** The Optimal Pattern: Separate Territory Base from Fort Chain

The key structural principle (Batches 8, 10): your territory base (the 5+
squares that enable fortification) should be geographically separate from your
fort chain (the path where you place forts).

Red's optimal example:
- Territory base: c1, b1, b2, c2, c3 (b/c-columns, rows 1-3)
- Fort chain: d3, d4, d5, c5, c4 (d-column + c-column upper, rows 3-5)
- Zero overlap: every fort is on enemy territory (self-fueling)

When territory base and fort chain overlap (self-consuming), each fort placed
risks dropping below the 5-territory threshold, forcing costly rebuilding.

** Territory Denial

Round 2 (Batch 9) extensively tested territory denial as a primary strategy.
The revised understanding:

*** Denial is a secondary effect, not a primary strategy

Pure denial (stepping on opponent territory without forting) wastes tempo. The
denier falls behind in the fort race. The hierarchy:

1. *Fort-denial* (S Tier): Place forts on opponent's territory. Scores AND denies.
2. *Incidental denial* (A Tier): Choose expansion paths through opponent territory.
   Free side effect, no tempo cost.
3. *Structural denial* (B Tier): Fort walls that permanently bisect the board.
   Powerful but expensive (3+ turns for a wall).
4. *Pure denial* (D Tier): Stepping on territory without forting. Temporary and
   costly. Only worthwhile to drop opponent below 5 before their first fort.

*** The Denial Hierarchy

| Strategy        | Beats           | Loses to         |
|-----------------+-----------------+------------------|
| Counter-denial  | Denial          | --               |
| Denial          | Fort-focus      | Counter-denial   |
| Fort-focus      | Passive         | Denial (pre-fort)|
| Passive         | --              | Everything       |

Counter-denial = reading the opponent's fort direction and blocking it with
your own fort. This costs 1 tempo but disrupts 2+ opponent turns.

*** When to Switch from Denial to Fortification

Switch to fortification IMMEDIATELY when you have 5+ territory, fort supply > 0,
and your position is on a good fort path. Never spend a turn on pure denial when
you could fort instead. Fort-denial (forting on enemy territory) is always
superior to stepping-on-territory denial because it scores AND denies.

* Counter-Strategies

** Countering BlueOpt (Resolved)

Round 1 left this as an open question. Round 2 provided the answer:

*Red =wn= beats BlueOpt without any special counter.*

The =wn= opening creates territory on the b-column, away from Blue's standard
burst paths. Red then pushes through d3->d4->d5 with through-territory forting,
denying Blue territory with every fort placed. BlueOpt's burst cannot overwrite
b-column territory efficiently.

The Batch 6 =nk= + =wne= (c3 block) counter is weaker (~55%) because:
- It requires banking, which loses tempo
- Blue can counter-counter with west detour (WSSE) through Red's b-column trail
- The block creates a critical vulnerability in Red's own territory

** Countering Red =wn= (Blue's Dilemma)

No Blue strategy consistently beats optimal Red =wn=. The best responses:

1. *SES* (best): c5->c4->d4->d3. Competitive but loses in 7 turns.
2. *SWS*: c5->c4->b4->b3. Loses because Red already holds b-column.
3. *Adaptive BlueOpt*: SK + burst. Slightly slower than SES against =wn=.
4. *Aggression*: Rush toward Red. Fails because Red forts while Blue chases.

Blue's win condition against =wn=: *Red must misplay.* Specifically, Red must
open =nn= (allowing Blue SWS) or delay fortification. Against perfect Red play,
Blue cannot win.

** Countering the Double-Fort (FEF)

Red's counter:
1. Read the direction: FEF goes east, FWF goes west.
2. Block at c4 with a fort, cutting Blue's northward column.
3. Fort into Blue's territory to drop them below 5.

** Countering Aggression

Counter with flanking: move away from the aggressor and build territory on the
opposite side. Games 41-45 (Batch 3) showed flanking winning 5/5 against
aggressive Red.

** Countering Banking

Place one fort per turn during the opponent's banking phase. A 3+ fort deficit
from banking is unrecoverable (confirmed Batch 8).

* Strategy Tier List (Final -- 200 Games)

** Red Strategies

*** S+ Tier
- =wn= + =en= + through-territory forting :: The optimal Red line. Fort chain
  runs through Blue's territory (d3->d4->d5->c5->c4). 7-turn wins. Beats every
  Blue response.

*** A Tier
- =ne= / =en= + adaptive forting :: Clean development, 7-8 turn wins. Slightly
  inferior to =wn= due to c-column overlap. Reliable against most Blue play.

*** B Tier
- =nn= + standard fort sweep :: Fast center control but *vulnerable to Blue SWS*.
  Red =nn= is the one opening where Blue can win consistently. Avoid unless you
  know Blue won't play SWS.
- Edge/Wall strategies (=ee=, =ww=) :: Efficient fort columns along board edges.
  Reliable but cedes the center.

*** C Tier
- Banking variants (=nk=) :: Loses tempo. The c3 block counter to BlueOpt has
  only ~55% win rate and is strictly worse than =wn=.
- Aggressive/Raider :: Disrupts opponent but wastes tempo chasing. Works against
  passive play, fails against competent opponents.

*** F Tier
- Defensive / passive :: Cedes all initiative. "Defense loses in Terratri."
- Center oscillation :: Trapped in infinite loops. Catastrophically bad.

** Blue Strategies

*** S Tier
- Standard SES against Red =wn= :: Best response to optimal Red. Loses in 7 but
  most competitive option. Creates territory on d-column.
- SWS against Red =nn= :: Beats Red's second-best opening. Blue's only reliable
  winning line at high-level play.

*** A Tier
- Adaptive BlueOpt (SK + directional burst) :: Strong against suboptimal Red.
  Choose burst direction based on Red's trail:
  - vs =nn=: SSWS (7-turn wins, fastest)
  - vs =en=: SSES (8 turns)
  - vs =wn=: WSSW (8 turns)
  Slightly slower than standard SES against =wn=.

*** B Tier
- Standard SSES BlueOpt :: The original BlueOpt. Beats most Red openings in 8
  turns. Less efficient than adaptive burst but simpler.
- Double-Fort FEF/FWF :: 2 forts in one turn. Viable against unprepared Red,
  countered by c4 blocking fort. Banking cost hurts at championship level.

*** C Tier
- Structural denial (c-column fort wall) :: Powerful defensive structure but
  expensive (3+ turns). Only worthwhile if Red doesn't read it and counter.

*** F Tier
- Maximum banking :: 5 banked = 0 fort supply. Always loses.
- Turtle / passive :: Wastes tempo. Even Random Red beats passive Blue.

* The Optimal Game

The verified optimal line (Game 200, confirmed move-by-move via MCP tool):

#+begin_example
Steps: wn|SES|en|SE|ef|NX|nf|NX|nf|FN|wf|SX|sf|
#+end_example

| Turn | Red                         | Blue                        |
|------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------|
|    1 | =wn= c1->b1->b2            | =SES= c5->c4->d4->d3       |
|    2 | =en= b2->c2->c3            | =SE= d3->d2->e2             |
|    3 | =ef= c3->d3, fort d3       | =NX= e2->e3, pass           |
|    4 | =nf= d3->d4, fort d4       | =NX= e3->e4, pass           |
|    5 | =nf= d4->d5, fort d5       | =FN= fort e4, e4->e5        |
|    6 | =wf= d5->c5, fort c5       | =SX= e5->e4, pass           |
|    7 | =sf= c5->c4, fort c4       | -- RED WINS (5 forts)       |

Key dynamics:
- Red's fort chain (d3, d4, d5, c5, c4) runs entirely through Blue's territory
- Each Red fort steals a Blue territory square (through-territory forting)
- Blue is pushed below 5 territory and can only manage 1 fort by Turn 5
- Red wins 5-1 in forts despite Blue playing competently

* Key Principles Summary (Revised)

1. *Red =wn= is optimal.* The universal best Red opening across 200 games.
2. *Separate territory base from fort chain.* Self-fueling forts (on enemy
   territory) are vastly superior to self-consuming forts (on your own).
3. *Fortify immediately at threshold.* One delayed fort can cost the game.
4. *Fort-move-fort-move.* The optimal endgame pattern. Every turn should place a fort.
5. *Through-territory forting wins games.* Build forts on your opponent's territory.
   Each fort scores AND denies -- a double advantage that compounds each turn.
6. *Blue cannot win against perfect Red.* Blue's goal is to exploit Red mistakes.
   The most common Red mistake is the =nn= opening (allows Blue SWS counter).
7. *Two forts behind is unrecoverable.* The maximum comeback is from 1 fort behind,
   and only by dropping the opponent below the 5-territory threshold.
8. *Banking is almost always wrong.* The only high-value bank is Blue SK against
   Red =nn=. Red should never bank. Additional Blue banking loses tempo.
9. *Territory denial is a side effect, not a strategy.* Fort-denial (forting on
   enemy territory) achieves denial for free. Pure denial wastes tempo.
10. *Read your opponent's opening.* The optimal Blue response depends entirely on
    Red's first move: SES against =wn=, SWS against =nn=, adaptive BlueOpt
    against suboptimal Red.

* Appendix: The Solved Game

Terratri appears to be a *solved game in Red's favor*. With optimal play:

- Red wins in exactly 7 turns (the theoretical minimum)
- Red places 5 forts while Blue manages only 1
- Blue cannot force a win from any position against optimal Red

The game's balance stands at approximately *75-25 in Red's favor* at
championship level. Blue can only win when Red plays suboptimally, particularly
the =nn= opening which allows the Blue SWS counter.

This makes Terratri structurally similar to other first-mover-advantage abstract
games (like Connect Four, where first player wins with perfect play). The
game remains interesting for human play because:
1. The =wn= + through-territory forting line must be known and executed precisely
2. Suboptimal Red openings create genuine Blue winning chances
3. The adaptive response tree (SES vs SWS vs BlueOpt) rewards reading your opponent
4. At casual skill levels, the game is balanced and dynamic

** Open Questions

Even after 200 games, some questions remain for future investigation:

1. *Is the optimal line truly forced?* Does Blue have a response to Red =wn=
   that performs better than SES? Could an unconventional Blue opening (e.g.,
   =EK= east-bank, or =WK= west-bank) surprise Red?
2. *Board size sensitivity.* Would Terratri on a 7x7 or 9x9 board change the
   Red advantage? Larger boards may give Blue more room to establish territory
   before Red's fort chain reaches them.
3. *Fort threshold sensitivity.* If the fortification threshold were 6 or 7
   instead of 5, would Blue have better chances? Higher thresholds slow the
   fort race and may reduce first-mover advantage.
4. *Alternative win conditions.* Could a territory-based alternative win
   condition (e.g., control 15+ squares) create more balanced gameplay?
