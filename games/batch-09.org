means actively moving onto opponent territory squares to reduce their count,
prevent fortification, and disrupt their plans.

* Game 161: Red wins in 7 turns
:PROPERTIES:
:STEPS: nn|SES|wn|SE|wf|FN|nf|FN|ef|FN|ef|WF|sf
:WINNER: red
:TURNS: 7
:RED_STRATEGY: Fort-Denial Sweep
:BLUE_STRATEGY: Smart
:END:

** Play-by-play
- Turn 1 (Red): =nn= -- c1->c2->c3. Standard center rush. tR=2.
- Turn 1 (Blue): =SES= -- c5->c4->d4->d3. BlueOpt opening using bank. tB=3.
- Turn 2 (Red): =wn= -- c3->b3->b4. Red expands west. tR=4.
- Turn 2 (Blue): =SE= -- d3->d2->e2. Blue reaches 5 territory.
- Turn 3 (Red): =wf= -- b4->a4, fort a4. tR=5. fR=1. Red starts fort column.
- Turn 3 (Blue): =FN= -- Fort e2, north e3. fB=1.
- Turn 4 (Red): =nf= -- a4->a5, fort a5. fR=2.
- Turn 4 (Blue): =FN= -- Fort e3, north e4. fB=2.
- Turn 5 (Red): =ef= -- a5->b5, fort b5. fR=3. Red begins the DENIAL SWEEP eastward.
- Turn 5 (Blue): =FN= -- Fort e4, north e5. fB=3.
- Turn 6 (Red): =ef= -- b5->c5, fort c5. DENIAL: c5 was Blue territory! fR=4. tB drops.
- Turn 6 (Blue): =WF= -- e5->d5, fort d5. fB=4.
- Turn 7 (Red): =sf= -- c5->c4, fort c4. DENIAL: c4 was Blue territory! fR=5. RED WINS!

Red's final two forts (c5 and c4) landed on Blue's territory, simultaneously denying Blue squares and scoring forts. This is the standard Game 81 pattern reframed as territory denial.

** Key moment
Turns 6-7: Red's eastward fort sweep through row 5 into c5/c4 was pure fort-denial. Each fort simultaneously scored a point AND stole Blue territory. Blue's c-column territory was permanently lost to Red's forts.

** Theory check
Fort-denial (fortifying on enemy territory) is the strongest and most efficient form of territory denial. It costs zero extra tempo because the fort move naturally claims the square. The standard Red sweep through row 5 includes 2-3 denial forts by default.

* Game 162: Red wins in 8 turns
:PROPERTIES:

:WINNER: red
:TURNS: 8

- Turn 2 (Blue): =WN= -- d3->c3->c4. Blue retreats to rebuild.

- Turn 4 (Blue): =FX= -- Fort b5. fB=1.

Invasion denial works best when the opponent leaves a trail of territory near your position. Blue's SES ending at d3 (adjacent to Red at d2) gave Red easy access to Blue's d4. Once Red captured it, the entire c-d column became Red's fort lane. The lesson: aggressive denial requires proximity to opponent territory.

* Game 163: Blue wins in 7 turns
:PROPERTIES:
:STEPS: en|WSX|en|SWX|nw|SEX|fx|FEF|ef|NF|nf|NF|wf|NF
:WINNER: blue
:TURNS: 7

- Turn 1 (Red): =en= -- c1->d1->d2. Red expands east. tR=2.
- Turn 1 (Blue): =WSX= -- c5->b5->b4, end. Blue preserves bank. tB=2.
- Turn 2 (Red): =en= -- d2->e2->e3. Red pushes deep east. tR=4(c1,d1,d2,e2).
- Turn 2 (Blue): =SWX= -- b4->b3->a3, end. Bank preserved. tB=4(c5,b5,b4,b3).
- Turn 3 (Red): =nw= -- e3->e4->d4. tR=6. Red reaches fort threshold.
- Turn 3 (Blue): =SEX= -- a3->a2->b2, end. tB=6. Bank=1.
- Turn 4 (Red): =fx= -- Fort d4. fR=1. Red attempts to deny by forting centrally.
- Turn 4 (Blue): =FEF= -- Double-fort: fort b2, east c2, fort c2. fB=2! Blue's FEF overcomes Red's denial attempt with tempo superiority.
- Turn 5 (Red): =ef= -- d4->e4, fort. fR=2. Red continues east-side forts.
- Turn 5 (Blue): =NF= -- c2->c3, fort c3. fB=3. Blue builds c-column.
- Turn 6 (Red): =nf= -- e4->e5, fort. fR=3.
- Turn 6 (Blue): =NF= -- c3->c4, fort c4. fB=4. Blue's denial wall blocks Red.
- Turn 7 (Red): =wf= -- e5->d5, fort. fR=4. Too late.
- Turn 7 (Blue): =NF= -- c4->c5, fort c5. fB=5! BLUE WINS!

Red's east-side expansion (denial of Blue's potential east territory) was irrelevant because Blue built the entire c-column from the west side. Red's denial targeted the wrong zone.

** Key moment
Red's denial was misdirected. Red went east (en|en|nw) to deny Blue's potential east-side territory, but Blue had already moved WEST (WSX|SWX|SEX). Red denied empty space while Blue built a winning fort column. The lesson: denial must target where the opponent IS, not where they might go.

** Theory check
Territory denial fails when the denier misjudges the opponent's expansion direction. Red expanded east (presuming Blue would go east too) but Blue went west, making Red's "denial" irrelevant. The FEF then gave Blue the tempo to build the c-column fort wall unopposed. Denial requires intelligence about the opponent's plans.

- Turn 3 (Red): =wn= -- c3->b3! DENIAL! b3 was Blue territory. Red steals it! b3->b4! DENIAL! b4 was Blue territory. tR=6(c1,d1,d2,d3,c3,b3). tB drops from 5 to 3(c5,c4,b2). Blue devastated!
- Turn 3 (Blue): =WS= -- c2->b2 (own territory), b2->b1. Blue retreats to rebuild. tB=4(c5,c4,c2,b2).
- Turn 4 (Red): =fx= -- Fort b4. fR=1. Fort on stolen Blue territory!

- Turn 6 (Red): =sf= -- b3->b2! DENIAL again! b2 was Blue '_'. Fort b2. fR=3. tB drops.

- Turn 7 (Red): =sf= -- b2->b1! b1 was Blue '_'. Fort b1. fR=4.

This is the Game 61 pattern -- Red invades Blue's b-column trail and builds the entire fort column on stolen territory.

** Key moment
Turn 3: Red's =wn= invasion (c3->b3->b4) was devastating. Red walked through two Blue territory squares, claiming both. From that foothold, Red built an entire fort column (b4-b3-b2-b1-a1) primarily on Blue's former territory. This is the "trail invasion" pattern: follow the opponent's trail and colonize it.

** Theory check
Trail invasion is the most aggressive form of territory denial. When an opponent leaves a connected trail of territory, the denier can walk through it, claiming each square, and then fortify on the captured squares. This is permanent denial -- forts can't be recaptured. Game 61's pattern is the archetype: Red invades Blue's west flank and builds the entire fort column there.

* Mini-analysis: Games 161-165

Red won 4/5 as territory denier (Game 163 Blue won via FEF counter). Key findings:

1. FORT-DENIAL is the strongest form: fortifying ON enemy territory denies permanently while scoring forts. (Games 161, 164, 165)
2. TRAIL INVASION (following opponent's territory trail and fortifying on it) is devastating when the trail is accessible. (Games 162, 164)
3. ROW-5 SWEEP naturally includes 2-3 denial forts as a side effect of optimal play. (Games 161, 165)
4. Blue's FEF double-fort is the primary counter to Red denial. The tempo advantage from FEF can overcome the territory lost to denial. (Game 163)
5. Denial is most efficient when it costs zero extra tempo -- i.e., when the denial target is along Red's optimal fort path anyway.

* Research Findings

** Is territory denial viable as a primary strategy?

YES, but with caveats. Territory denial is viable when:
1. Combined with fortification (fort-denial, not pure denial)
2. Timed before the opponent reaches 5 territory
3. Targeting territory the opponent needs for fortification

Pure denial (stepping on territory without forting) is NOT viable as a primary strategy because it costs tempo without scoring forts. The denier falls behind in the fort race.

The strongest form of denial is INCIDENTAL: choose fort locations that happen to be on opponent territory. This costs nothing extra and achieves denial as a side effect.

Evidence: Games where denial preceded fortification (161, 162, 164, 176) resulted in wins. Games where denial replaced fortification (attempted in early Game 163 analysis) failed because the denier couldn't maintain fort pace.

** Which side is better at denial?

RED is better at transient denial (stepping on Blue's territory) due to first-mover advantage. Red can invade Blue's SES trail at Turn 2, before Blue reaches 5 territory.

BLUE is better at structural denial (fort walls) due to the FEF double-fort. Blue's c-column fort wall (c2-c3-c4-c5) bisects the board and blocks Red's movement permanently.

Red denial is disruptive but temporary. Blue denial is slower but permanent. The counter: Red plants a blocking fort (c4) to sever Blue's column.

Evidence: Red won 4/5 as transient denier (161-165). Blue won 3/5 as structural denier (166-170). Red's counter-denial (Game 170, 179) defeats Blue's structural denial.

** Denial vs fort-focus: which wins?

DENIAL beats FORT-FOCUS when denial drops the focuser below 5 territory before their first fort (Games 164, 176). The 3+ turn delay is insurmountable.

FORT-FOCUS beats DENIAL when the focuser starts forting before the denier can invade (Game 177 -- both sides fort at the same pace, Red's tempo wins).

FORT-FOCUS + COUNTER-DENIAL beats DENIAL (Games 170, 179). The counter involves reading the opponent's fort direction and blocking it.

The hierarchy: Counter-Denial > Denial > Fort-Focus > passive play.

** When should a player switch from denial to fortification?

Switch to fortification IMMEDIATELY when:
1. You have 5+ territory
2. Fort supply > 0
3. Your current position is on the optimal fort path

NEVER spend a turn on pure denial when you could fort instead. Fort-denial (forting on enemy territory) is always superior to stepping-on-territory denial because it scores forts and denies simultaneously.

The optimal decision tree:
- Can I fort? -> Fort (choose enemy territory if possible for denial bonus)
- Can't fort yet? -> Expand toward 5 territory (choose enemy territory if adjacent)
- Opponent about to fort? -> Invade their territory to drop them below 5

** Revised territory denial theory

ORIGINAL THEORY: Move onto opponent territory to reduce their count, blocking forts.

REVISED THEORY: Territory denial is a SECONDARY effect of optimal fort play, not a primary strategy.

Principles:
1. FORT-DENIAL > PURE DENIAL. Always prefer placing a fort on enemy territory over merely stepping on it. Forts are permanent denial; stepping is temporary.
2. INCIDENTAL DENIAL is free. The standard Red sweep through row 5 naturally denies 2-3 Blue squares as a side effect. This is the most efficient denial.
3. STRUCTURAL DENIAL (fort walls) is more powerful than transient denial. Blue's c-column fort wall is permanent and blocks movement. Red must counter with blocking forts.
4. TIMING is critical. Denial before the opponent's first fort is devastating. Denial after forts are placed is mostly irrelevant (forts can't be stolen).
5. COUNTER-DENIAL beats denial. The counter: plant a blocking fort in the opponent's planned fort path. This costs one tempo but disrupts 2+ opponent turns.
6. ADAPTIVE PLAY is optimal. The best strategy is fort-focus with tactical denial: always building toward 5 forts, but choosing fort locations that deny opponent territory when possible.
